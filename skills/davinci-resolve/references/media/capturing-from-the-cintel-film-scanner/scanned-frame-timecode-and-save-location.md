### Reset the Timecode

Establishing timecode for the film roll you are about to scan begins with finding that roll's zero frame. Convention calls for punching a tiny physical hole inside the frame that sits just ahead of the first usable frame on a roll, which then serves as a fixed landmark every time that roll gets scanned. The names for it are the marker frame, lab roll hole, or head punch. Anchoring the very first timecode frame to that marker frame means later passes of the same roll keep an identical frame count to earlier passes, so the same footage can be rescanned and reconformed any time the need arises. To reset scanned timecode right at a new roll's marker frame: 1 Drive the transport controls beneath the viewer over to where the marker frame sits. 2 Open the 'viewer' option menu and pick 'current frame timecode.'

Choosing Current Frame Timecode from the Viewer Option menu 3 Type a timecode figure into the dialog box that pops up. As an example, the opening roll of a project might take 01:00:00:00.

The Set Current Frame Timecode dialog 4 Once that's settled, press OK. Because timecode can never go negative, avoid setting the opening frame to zero. A second widespread housekeeping habit is to bump the hour digit each time you swap rolls, lining it up with the film roll's number, which makes pairing any scanned clip back to its source roll and frame range straightforward. Your Cintel Scanner ships with built-in 'Options Interface' ports so you can bolt on extra hardware down the road. That opens the door to extras like reading KeyKode off the camera negative, or handling optical/magnetic audio. Further detail lives in the 'Optional Audio and KeyKode Reader' section of the Blackmagic Cintel manual.

### Choose a Location to Save the Scanned Frames

With everything above sorted, scroll down to the 'capture info' controls inside DaVinci Resolve's film scanner panel, then press the 'browse' button to pick a destination for the scanned files. The remaining fields in this area let you decide which prefix gets attached to the names of the scanned files and their enclosing folders. The 'file name prefix' refreshes the file name preview displayed up top in the header. That header likewise reports the file path, resolution, frame rate, duration, and the format. Indicate which roll, reel, clip, and program details should travel with the scanned media. By default the 'timestamp prefix' checkbox in the 'Capture info' controls is on, and it tucks your clips into separate sub-folders inside the destination folder while stamping a timecode prefix onto the file name. Should you prefer every clip pooled together in a single master destination folder, just clear that checkbox. Whenever you grab an HDR clip, the scanner runs a high exposure scan and stashes it in a hidden folder called .HDR sitting alongside the standard scan. Remove the .HDR folder and the scan turns into an ordinary clip once you refresh it in media storage and re-import the clip into media pool. That comes in handy when the HDR portion of the scan is flawed, since you can readily turn it into a plain CRI clip.

### Check the Codec

DaVinci Resolve picks the 'Cintel Raw' codec on its own, though you may instead select 'Cintel Raw 3:1'.

### The Cintel Raw Format

The Cintel Raw Format Bayer pattern of every film frame your Cintel scanner's sensor records is stored, with scanner metadata baked in, as a 12-bit linear Cintel Raw Image — CRI for short — image sequence. During grading in DaVinci Resolve, CRI frames get debayered automatically into 12-bit log encoded image data. That logarithmic encoding resembles Cineon encoding without being a match for it. As an illustration, negative film carries a Gamma of 2.046 for density, whereas print film uses a full range Gamma 2.2 curve so that no image data ends up clipped. Either logarithmic encoding can be moved into a linear color space through the 'Cintel to Linear' 1D LUT before you push on toward whatever other color spaces you intend to work in. Scanning uses the entire sensor area of 4096x3072 in order to keep the audio waveform on view for optical audio and to leave room for perforation visibility used in stabilization. The frame is afterward cropped, and the resolution of the capture files turns on the source film format once overscan for perforations and the audio region have been stripped away. For a deeper look at scanning resolutions across film types, consult the 'specifications' section. By default the Cintel scanner writes Cintel Raw files using variable bitrate lossless compression. That compression is visually lossless and trims file size by roughly 3:2 depending on what the image holds. Cintel Raw 3:1 by contrast applies lossy compression at a ratio near 3:1. Quality stays very high there, yet it may not always be visually lossless. As an example, 35mm 4 perf files run about 12.5MB under Cintel Raw and about 6.3MB under Cintel Raw 3:1. For 16mm, files are roughly 4MB under Cintel Raw and roughly 2MB under Cintel Raw 3:1.

### CinemaDNG Quality Settings

The quality of CRI files is governed by the 'decode quality' and 'play quality' CinemaDNG settings found in the Camera Raw panel of the project Settings. Both default to 'full'. Where a machine is short on processor or memory headroom, you can drop these settings, but doing so degrades the quality of the final render.

### Set the Timeline Resolution

DaVinci Resolve shows and renders whatever the scanner outputs at the timeline's own resolution. Take 35mm 4 perforation film, which needs a custom resolution of 4096x3072 to hit maximum resolution. NOTE: Where your timeline runs HDR while the intended deliverable sits at Ultra HD, you may give up some resolution.

For the cropped image area resolutions across every film gauge, look to the 'effective resolutions' within the 'specifications' section. Otherwise, to read the captured clip's full native resolution, open the 'clips attributes' in DaVinci Resolve.

### Adjusting the Color of the Scanner

DaVinci Resolve's film scanner panel hands you command over the exposure and color temperature of the lamp lighting the film during scanning. The light source master wheel and RGB controls are how you tune these, the aim being to pull as much information as you can from each frame while keeping any region of the image from being clipped past recovery. Although CRI genuinely is a raw image format, there is no headroom outside the internal data range that DaVinci relies on, so stay aware that clipping data in the built-in video scopes during scanning can mean it's clipped for good in the scanned media. The frequency with which you tweak the color and exposure of scanned shots hinges on how varied the scenes are on a given film roll. Take the case of a roll holding many takes of one scene, all lit the same and all able to share one set of adjustments. Another roll might instead hold an assortment of scenes lit very differently from one another, forcing you to dial in each scanned clip on its own to get the most data quality. This matters because the light source master wheel and RGB controls won't shift on their own from one scanned clip to the next in a log and capture workflow. Practically, the present light source settings carry over to every clip you scan until you change them by hand again, even for clips you logged out of different stretches of a film roll. So the log and capture way of working only makes sense when it's reasonable to log a batch of clips that all share the same light source master wheel and RGB control adjustments. Failing that, the advice is to set your lighting on a clip by clip basis as each clip is scanned, wherever top image quality is needed for finishing. Bear in mind the purpose of these adjustments is to wring the most image data out of the scan, not to craft the clips' final look, which you'll build afterward in the grading phase using the controls of the 'color' page. To set the light source, pick a representative frame for that stretch of roll or for the opening run of shots you intend to scan, then adjust the light source as you watch the built-in video scopes. Move the light source master wheel to govern the intensity of the lamp lighting the film, lifting or dropping the R, G, and B channels together. On a typical camera negative, this is how you place the black point of the film image. Within a negative print, the image's darkest region maps to the highlights of the film image. Park the light source master wheel just over the customary Dmin value of 95, read off the histogram of the video scopes, which assures the highlights escape clipping during the Cineon LOG conversion DaVinci runs to debayer the CRI image for grading. With positive film, set the light source levels by hand so that no slice of the highlights or shadows in the signal gets clipped; typically 1000 in 10-bit or 4000 in 12-bit. Switching on 'show reference levels' within the waveform, RGB parade, or histogram scopes is also possible, and you'd then point the 'low' value at the digital Dmin value of 95. Once that's set, work the RGB controls to rebalance the three color channels by differing amounts, shifting the color temperature of the lamp lighting the film toward the most usable, or neutral, color balance in the scanned output.

### Scanning One or More Sections of Film

Having set the light source, it pays to keep things tidy as you scan each clip by typing every relevant detail into the metadata editor along the way. Within the 'capture info' group of metadata fields you'll find slots covering the file name prefix, roll, reel number, clip number, program name, flags, plus a marker for whether the take counts as good. Fill these fields ahead of scanning a clip and that metadata gets written into the clip. Down at the foot of the 'capture info' panel sit four buttons for film scanning.

Once that's all squared away, four routes are open to you for scanning the film:

- Capture Now: Reach for the capture now button when you want to pull long stretches of a reel in a single pass. Pressing 'capture now' starts scanning around the current frame and runs until you press 'stop'. With 'Enable 2 Pass HDR Scan' on, press 'Capture HDR' after the capture is underway so DaVinci Resolve understands you've hit the end of the clip you want and can move on to the high exposure pass. Scan the whole reel without ever pressing 'Capture HDR' and the scanner just carries on with the high intensity scan from your starting point through to the reel's end.
- Capture Clip: A tighter way to scan particular stretches of film. After the transport controls and the In and Out button have let you bound a stretch of film, a press of 'capture clip' scans that lone clip and then stops. With 'Enable 2 Pass HDR Scan' on, the high intensity HDR scan reuses the same In and Out points as the first scan.
- Batch Clips: This lets you log a number of clips ahead of time and then scan them together in one run using the current light source settings from DaVinci Resolve's film scanner panel. Log each clip beforehand by marking In and Out points for every stretch of film you mean to scan, then press the 'log clip' button to store that frame range as an unscanned clip in the media pool. Press 'batch clips' and every unscanned clip gets scanned in turn until the job finishes. You can also pick one or more unscanned clips, in which case only the chosen clips get scanned. On top of that, you can bring in an EDL tied to a specific film roll and feed the logged clips it produces into scanning.

NOTE: Pressing the 'log clip' button has Cintel Scanner hand every clip in the batch the same project settings, drawing on the latest project settings as of capture time. The recommendation is to verify the scanner settings ahead of kicking off the batch capture. With 'Enable 2 Pass HDR Scan' on, the high intensity HDR scan draws on the same In and Out point sets as that opening batch did. To dig deeper into batch capture workflows, turn to Chapter 24, "Ingesting From Tape."

- Snapshot: Capture a single frame with normal exposure and current scanner settings.

While a scan is running, should DaVinci Resolve find that your storage bandwidth can't keep up with the chosen speed, the scan speed drops on its own to keep the capture from failing. When the optional Audio and KeyKode Reader accessory is in use, the audio sample rate likewise shifts to hold onto your selected audio quality.

### Extracting Audio

When the film being scanned also carries an optical sound track, you can pull the audio out as a separate operation. While pulling the audio, DaVinci automatically compensates for a standing offset between picture and sound — 26 frames on 16mm, 21 frames on 35mm. Pick every clip carrying an optical sound track, then right-click one of the chosen clips and choose 'extract audio'. Resolve studies the overlapping optical track region of each frame and builds a matching audio track on its own, in sync with the scanned image sequence. The audio for each clip gets extracted automatically, embedded into the clip, and saved to the very directory the scanned frames were written to. A little audio icon shows up in the corner of your clip's thumbnail to signal there's a paired audio file.

To ease extraction, filter what's in media storage — by name, by resolution, by date modified, or down to film clips only. Narrowing the clips this way helps you home in on and grab precisely what's needed. You may also grab a wide selection and pull audio from several clips together by right clicking your selection and picking 'extract audio…' from the menu. As audio extraction runs, an information box reports the progress. Press the 'stop' button whenever you like to halt the extraction.

You can filter what's in media storage to make handling it easier. If the 'timestamp prefix' checkbox was cleared in the 'capture info' section back when your clips were scanned and you want extracted audio embedded into your clips automatically, be sure to always run the audio extraction on the clips from inside the media pool.
