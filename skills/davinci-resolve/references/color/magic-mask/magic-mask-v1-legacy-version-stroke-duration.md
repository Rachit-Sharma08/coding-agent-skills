### Stroke Duration

A freshly drawn stroke starts out lasting only one frame, sitting at the timecode where the playhead was parked as you drew it. Nudge the playhead either direction and the stroke vanishes. To have one or more strokes keep examining the subject across an entire shot, you must reach for the Motion Tracking controls and track the stroke so it travels with whatever it sits on. Tracking a stroke stretches its duration until it spans the full range of frames that were tracked. Should a stroke eventually become pointless, for instance once a person exits the frame, you can halt its tracking once it is no longer needed and it ceases to feed the analysis. That behavior helps, because frequently you will be dropping strokes to address analysis glitches that persist only briefly, patching a momentary gap in the mask. Bear in mind, though, that drawing a stroke and immediately jumping to the next shot is not enough; for a subject to stay masked continuously, at least one stroke must be tracked across the whole time that subject remains visible.

### Tracking Strokes to Follow Subject Motion

Picture every stroke you have placed as a persistent eyedropper that keeps sampling whatever portion of the image sits beneath it. From the combined analysis of all the strokes, a mask is produced live across every frame of the clip. Consequently, when the camera or subject shifts, each stroke has to be motion tracked or otherwise repositioned to ride along with that movement, keeping the subject properly recognized. It also falls to you to confirm each stroke can actually analyze its intended target, and to switch off any stroke that, for any reason, cannot. TIP: Because Magic Mask demands a lot from the processor, an option called Use Fast Tracking lives in the Magic Mask palette’s Option menu so that people on weaker workstations can speed tracking up, the trade-off being possibly rougher results on subjects that move quickly or unpredictably.

### Methods of Moving Strokes to Follow Subject Motion

Given all of the above and then some, several different approaches exist for working strokes to sharpen the final analysis.

- Motion Tracking: At the top of the Magic Mask palette sits a group of tracking controls that let you motion track one or more strokes so they trail the camera and subject motion within a clip. In effect, each tracked frame receives a motion-tracked reference frame that shifts the stroke to a fresh position.
- Reference Frame: Whenever you drag a stroke away from where it started in the Timeline, you generate a static reference frame at that frame which immediately jumps the stroke from its old spot to the new one at that frame. Since these behave much like static keyframes, no motion is interpolated between one “keyframe” and the next to build animation. Rather, a reference frame inside the Magic Mask palette is just a plain frame-by-frame action. Each time you reposition a stroke at a fresh frame, you reset the stroke’s placement from that frame onward, leaving its position untouched on any earlier frame that was already tracked.
- If that strikes you as crude, remember that producing elegant stroke animation is not the point of this tool. All you have to do is shift each stroke so it stays on whatever subject or background feature it is meant to sample in a given frame. What truly counts is that, on every frame, the stroke covers a fitting portion of the feature you are isolating so the right mask forms. Should the stroke leap about erratically, that is irrelevant provided the resulting mask is sound. So even when you are reduced to a bit of frame-by-frame work to keep a stroke pinned to an awkward feature (a veil flapping in the breeze, say), the job moves quickly, since on each frame you are merely dragging the stroke to land on the feature wherever it has drifted. That dragging need not be smooth, only correct.
- Combining Tracking and Reference Frames: Because every motion-tracked frame is effectively keyframed, hand-dragging a stroke on a frame that has already been tracked replaces the tracking data there with a reference frame holding the stroke’s new placement at that frame. When no further tracking data exists, whether ahead of or behind that reference frame, the stroke vanishes until you track some more. When tracking data sits directly after a reference frame, the stroke snaps to the spot that tracking data calls for.
- Deleting Stroke Tracking at Specific Frames: For situations where the subject you are isolating slips behind something and stays hidden across a span of frames, you are given the means to delete stroke tracking on one or more frames. With that, you can wipe out the stroke across however many frames the subject you are tracking is hidden and cannot be sampled. This simplifies handling features that appear and vanish throughout a clip, since you can delete stroke

tracking across as many spans of frames as the feature is absent. Mute some frames by accident and you can simply undo or re-track the stroke through them. Deleting tracking frames is a matter of drawing a bounding box on the stroke tracks across the span you want gone, then picking Clear Selected Track Data from the Magic Mask option menu.

### Option Menu Commands for Removing Strokes and Tracking

A handful of commands in the Magic Mask palette’s Option menu let you wipe out either strokes themselves or their stroke tracking data.

- Clear All Strokes: Wipes out the Stoke list entirely, tracking included.
- Clear All Strokes of Current Frame: Removes the tracking data sitting at the playhead’s present spot

on all strokes in the list.

- Clear All Strokes from In to Out: Should you use the I and O keys to mark In and Out points in the Stroke

Timeline, this command wipes every stroke’s tracking data inside that range.

- Clear Selected Stroke from In to Out: Pick a stroke in the Stroke list, then use the I

and O keys to mark the In along with Out points inside the Stroke Timeline, then this command clears the tracking data on whichever strokes are selected across that span.

- Clear Selected Track Data: Drag a bounding box around one or more strokes’ tracking data and this command removes every bit of tracking data inside the box.

### An Example Stroke Tracking Workflow

Blending motion tracking, hand-placed reference frames, and stroke muting lets you knock out the isolation of most moving subjects in short order. The walkthrough that follows shows one way to combine these methods so the strokes you have drawn ride along with the features you are isolating. To make one or several strokes trail a subject: 1 If needed, park the playhead on the frame where you want to start isolating your chosen subject. Say you are isolating a person stepping through a doorway into a room; you would likely start your work on a frame where that person already stands inside, then proceed outward in both directions from there. When your subject is already fully in view on the shot’s opening frame, you can begin right there. 2 Draw at least one stroke marking the subject or feature you mean to isolate. Tracking a stroke to follow the features is impossible without at least one stroke present. 3 Decide whether to track every stroke or only the selected ones. By default, every existing stroke that has at least one frame in the Stroke Timeline at the playhead’s spot gets tracked. You can, however, opt to track only the strokes selected in the Stroke list by picking Track Selected Stroke Only from the Magic Mask palette’s Option menu. 4 Press the Track Forwards to End or Track Backwards to Beginning buttons to motion track the strokes over whatever remains of the clip’s duration. By default each stroke tracks against whatever image detail sits right around it; defining a tracking region is unnecessary, since this happens on its own. While tracking runs, each stroke’s tracking bar fills in to indicate which frames are done and which remain. At the same time, every stroke under tracking will reshape to follow basic position and rotation shifts in the subject. That said, anything that hides the subject, along with more drastic motion, can create trouble. Any stroke tracking a feature that exits the screen is automatically dropped from tracking on the frame where that feature is fully gone, even should the feature reappear on a later frame.

5 (Optional) Should a problem arise mid-track, such as the subject being hidden behind another object in the frame (for instance, a person’s face passing behind a tree as they walk), press the Pause Track button. The following options exist for handling track problems: a) If a stroke has completely moved off of the subject: Suppose you are tracking a stroke on a person and they spin around 180 degrees, sending the stroke off onto the background. The fix: pause the track, move the playhead to the earliest frame where the stroke stopped tracking the subject correctly, then drag the stroke with the pointer onto another clearly visible portion of the subject. Every frame on which you drag the stroke somewhere new automatically gains a fresh reference frame. Keep this up until the subject’s motion settles down enough to track again, at which point you can resume tracking. b) If a stroke drifts onto an object that partly hides the subject: Pause the track, draw a bounding box across the troublesome frames, and pick Delete Selected Tracking from the Magic Mask Option menu to scrub the bad tracking data. Next, move the playhead to the first frame of that deleted tracking data, and drag the stroke with the pointer onto whatever part of the subject is visible on either side of the obstruction. Advance the playhead frame by frame, dragging the stroke onto whichever portion of the subject peeks out from behind the obstruction, until the subject clears it. From there you can press the Track Forwards to End or Track Backwards to Beginning buttons and carry on tracking as before. c) If the subject becomes completely hidden: Clips in which the subject comes and goes across the clip’s length can be handled by deleting the stroke or strokes on that subject across every frame where it is hidden, so the strokes only sample the image while the subject is visible and therefore isolatable. You can wipe one or more strokes by dragging a bounding box across a span of frames, then choosing the Magic Mask Option menu’s Clear Selected Track Data command. d) If a stroke struggles to stay on the subject: When the subject you are tracking moves too quickly, too erratically, or warps its shape too drastically for the tracker to do well (for example, a person raising an arm toward the lens), you can work through the troublesome stretch of clip one frame at a time with the Track Forward 1 Frame or Track Backward 1 Frame buttons, dragging the stroke by hand to stay on the subject as you advance. Once the difficult motion ends and the subject moves more steadily again, you can press the Track Forwards to End or Track Backwards to Beginning buttons and continue tracking as before. e) If the subject is moving off the frame: Suppose you placed a stroke in the center of someone’s face and that face is drifting off screen; you can hand-reposition the stroke across the final few frames before the subject fully departs, so the stroke keeps marking whatever parts of the face remain visible even as the area it originally followed disappears. After the face leaves the frame entirely, you can mute the stroke for the remainder of the shot. 6 (Optional) When you have only tracked a stroke’s movement across part of a shot, you can press the Jump to Start or Jump to End buttons to send the playhead to the first or last tracked frame, getting ready to track onward from there.
