### Classic Stabilizing Using the Point Tracker

When a clip has so many moving subjects that the Cloud Tracker can’t hold a steady lock, you’ll have to track one very particular feature to pull off a clean stabilization. Under those conditions, reaching for the Point Tracker to stabilize is frequently the quicker route. To stabilize an image using the Point Tracker: 1 Bring up the Tracker palette and pick Stabilizer out of the Palette mode drop-down menu. Then pick Classic Stabilizer from the option menu. 2 Pick Point Tracker from the Tracker Type drop-down menu over at the lower right of the Tracker palette. Doing that swaps out the controls shown beneath the tracker graph. 3 Press the Add Tracker Point button to drop a crosshairs onto the Viewer, then drag it onto a high-contrast, angular feature you’d like to track — one that represents how the clip moves overall.

### Using the Point Tracker to stabilize an image

TIP: If you like, you can drop in more than one Tracker crosshairs to follow several features at once, and DaVinci Resolve will try to stabilize across as many dimensions as those extra trackers permit. A single point gets you Pan and Tilt stabilization. Two points let you attempt Pan, Tilt, and Rotation. Four points let you attempt Pan, Tilt, Rotation, and Zoom. 4 Press the Track Forward button to walk the clip forward (or Track Reverse if you’d sooner begin at the clip’s tail and move back). This studies the feature you marked with the crosshairs, though as yet no stabilization gets applied. 5 Set the Strong parameter to match the sort of stabilization you’re after. To strip every trace of motion out of the shot, keep Strong at 100. To use stabilization for taking the edge off the shot while still leaving a bit of motion in frame, pull the strong parameter below 100 yet above 0. Because this parameter acts as a multiplier, larger figures mean a greater share of smoothing gets applied across the clip’s range of motion, while smaller figures mean a smaller share of smoothing gets applied across the original range of motion.

6 Having dialed the Strong parameter under 100 to settle the shot’s motion instead of locking it down outright, you can additionally turn to the Smooth parameter to mathematically even out whatever motion is left over. That gives you a way to flatten unwanted “wobbling” while holding on to the camera movement you’d like to keep. Since the Strong and Smooth parameters operate in tandem, try out a range of values across both until the outcome suits you. 7 Decide how the edges of the stabilized clip ought to be treated by way of the Zoom checkbox:

- Should you want DaVinci Resolve to push into the image as far as it takes to keep blanking (black edges) from creeping into the frame (a side effect of shifting the image to even out camera motion), then switch on the Viewer Zoom checkbox.
- Should you prefer the image scale untouched, letting blanking (black edges) creep into the frame so that you can either (a) hand the job of patching those gaps to an effects artist down the line, or (b) keyframe the Input Sizing Zoom parameter yourself to remove blanking dynamically, then switch off the Viewer Zoom checkbox.

8 Once all of those controls are set, press Stabilize. Right away the clip gets reshaped to deliver whatever degree of stabilization you dialed in through the Smooth Frames slider. 9 Run the clip and look over how the stabilization turned out. Should adjustments be in order, pick fresh Strong and Smooth Frames values and press Stabilize once more. Each time you alter any of the Stabilization parameters, pressing Stabilize is required so the resulting transform on your clip gets recomputed.

### Using Stabilization to Create a Match Move

There are times — for example while you’re leaning on the HSL Qualifier to build transparency for a composite (by way of the Alpha Output covered later in this chapter) — when you’ll need to transfer the movement of a foreground clip onto the background clip so the pair read as though they move together. The term for this is a “match move,” and it’s an essential step when assembling a composite for something like a sky replacement. Getting there is straightforward by way of the Stabilizer mode within the Tracker palette. To line a background clip’s movement up with that of a foreground clip: 1 To start, over in the Edit page, cut a foreground clip onto track V2 and a background clip carrying a more eye-catching sky onto track V1. 2 That accomplished, head to the Color page. Follow the walkthrough found under the heading “Using a Qualifier Key to Create Transparency” — that material lives in Chapter 147, “Channel Splitting and Image Compositing,” — so as to key out the sky, then put that key to work carving a transparent region in the foreground clip so the background sky clip on track V1 peeks through.

Here the flat blue sky gets keyed out so a more striking shot of a dramatically cloudy sky can stand in for it.

Before and after building a sky replacement by routing an HSL qualifier of the sky’s blue into the Alpha Output to make transparency This same approach works just as well whether you’re dealing with still photos or moving footage. On top of that, the amount of motion you intend to match usually means you’ll want a background image a good deal bigger than the area you’re keying, guaranteeing full coverage as the layer shifts to follow the foreground motion. Where needed, push into the image with the Zoom parameter over in the Transform section of the Edit page Inspector. 3 In this case the foreground clip carries a camera pan, and that tips off the viewer to the composite because the sky stays still instead of moving with it. To slide the compound clip holding the sky image so it follows that pan, open the Tracker pallet. 4 Select its Stabilizer mode, then press the Analyze Forward button so the motion of the whole clip is analyzed. After that, open the Tracker palette’s Option menu and pick Copy Track Data. 5 Highlight the clip you’re aiming to match (here that’s the sky image), then open the Tracker Palette’s Option menu and pick Paste Track Data. 6 For this to behave properly, you’ll need to dial the Strong parameter to –100, then press the Stabilize button. Feeding in a negative value flips the tracking data you grabbed back in step 3, turning it into a match move. And if you’re in the mood for a clever touch, nudge the Strong parameter just shy of –100, say –94, building in a slight mismatch between foreground and background motion that fakes parallax.
