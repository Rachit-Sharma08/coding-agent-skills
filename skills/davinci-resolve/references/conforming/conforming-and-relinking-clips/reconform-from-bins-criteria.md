### The Reconform from Bins dialog

That said, when the criteria you've picked to drive the conform fail to match, the Reconform From Bins operation falls over, and you'll have to either retry with different conform criteria or swap the required clips into the Timeline by hand. Here's a walkthrough one step at a time. The procedure for reconforming a timeline to clips held in one particular Media Pool bin runs like this: 1 To open it, Double-click the Timeline that's to be reconformed. 2 Single out just the clips you intend to reconform, or press Command-A so the whole Timeline is selected when you'd sooner reconform throughout it rather than choosing clips individually. 3 On one of the chosen clips, Right-click and pick “Conform Lock Enabled” so as to disable Conform Lock Enabled across the clips bound for reconforming. That frees DaVinci Resolve to consider every viable conform match wherever a handful of clips may share overlapping timecode within the Media Pool. 4 On the current Timeline in the Media Pool, Right-click and head to Timelines > Reconform From Bins. The Conform Options dialog surfaces; its left side carries Timeline Options plus the Choose Conform Bins list, while the Conform Options panel occupies the right. 5 Within the Timeline Options section, decide between conforming to All Clips and only to Selected Clips. After that, decide whether the option to “Set clips to Conform Lock Enabled” should apply once conform finishes.

Choosing which clips in the Timeline to attempt to reconform 6 Over in the Choose Conform Bins section, click that disclosure triangle to the left of the Master bin so the sub-bins nested inside come into view. 7 Switch on the checkboxes belonging to whichever bins hold media the Timeline should conform to, and switch off the checkboxes for any bins whose media you'd rather leave out.

Selecting folders for reconform 8 Then settle on the conform options you'd like weighed while timeline clips are matched against Media Pool clips in the chosen bins. Timecode is on by default. Sequence is what counts, because DaVinci Resolve begins conforming clips at the leftmost format/codec and steps rightward to the following format/codec whenever no match appears, working through every entry you listed. To clear that badge, you can pick either just the conformed clips or every clip in the Timeline, right-click them, and choose Conform Lock Enabled to clear these warnings.

### Using Reconform From Media Storage

DaVinci Resolve 14 adds a second reconform method, which, through the “Reconform From Media Storage Folders” command, conforms a timeline's clips to clips housed in one specific File System directory (its subdirectories included). This lets you reconform many clips in a timeline in one shot, matching them to source media files on disk without first importing those clips into the Media Pool; whatever clips can be conformed under the stated conform criteria get imported automatically as needed. A key trait of the Reconform From Media Storage command is that DaVinci Resolve reconforms every timeline clip it can pair with source media files on disk in the chosen Media Storage directories, while leaving alone any Timeline clips it cannot match. That makes Reconform From Media Storage a great command for the situations below:

- When clips scattered through an existing timeline must be reconformed down to a narrower slice of media housed in one particular on-disk directory — for instance, refreshed Motion Graphics or VFX delivered by a third-party application.
- When missing timeline clips throughout an imported timeline need a fast reconform, and especially when custom conform criteria are required to reconform those clips successfully. (Unlinked clips reconform under this command only if you first select them and switch off Conform Lock Enabled.)

As with the Reconform From Bins command, you get to spell out precisely which blend of conform criteria should pair clips in the timeline with clips in the Media Pool. So you aren't held to just Timecode, Reel Name, and File Name; you may also reach, in whatever mix you like, for Resolution, Frame Rate, Bit Depth, File Format, Total Duration, Media UMID/UID, and/or Codec, steering how clips conform to suit your needs and whatever problem you're solving.

### The Reconform From Media Storage dialog

This route to timeline conform shines when the sole way to conform a timeline to the media you're after relies on a highly specific blend of metadata that strays from the rules DaVinci Resolve falls back on. Suppose, for instance, that your hard drive holds a tangled assortment of 8- and 10-bit versions of the same clips, yet you want a given timeline conformed only to the 10-bit media ahead of finishing. Reaching for “Reconform from media storage folders” lets you be exactly that precise about which media to use. That lets DaVinci Resolve entertain all feasible conform matches in spots where multiple clips could carry overlapping timecode in the Media Pool. 4 Right-click whichever timeline is to be reconformed, then go to Timelines > Reconform from media storage folders. ### Understanding, Fixing, and Using Reel Conflicts

Provided the “Auto conform clips with media added into Media Pool” setting remains enabled within the General Options panel of the Project Settings, that very live tie between clips in the Media Pool and a timeline's clips persists no matter whether the clips are linked or unlinked — there's no difference. The downside is that any time the Media Pool holds two separate versions of one clip, or for that matter two entirely unrelated clips that share a file name (or reel name) along with the identical overlapping timecode, DaVinci Resolve is free to conform to whichever clip by itself. Imagine, for example, that a clip's camera raw version sits in the Media Pool together with a ProRes or MXF transcoded version of it. The two clips carry the same content, the same file name, and the same span of frames. That sets up what DaVinci Resolve calls a “clip conflict.” It may slip past you at first because, out of the box, every clip imported alongside a timeline, or that you've cut into a brand new timeline, arrives with a Conform Lock Enabled setting switched on by default. Each clip in a timeline whose Conform Lock Enabled is on treats only the Media Pool clip it's currently conformed to as the valid one; all other clips whose file names and overlapping timecode would otherwise qualify as a valid match are disregarded. But right-click such a clip in the Timeline and switch Conform Lock Enabled off, and that clip throws up a “clip conflict” error, with an “attention” badge sitting left of its name in the Timeline.

Conflict icon flagging that two or more clips carry matching conform parameters Clip conflicts usually count as an error, though not in every case. They can bite when the media you brought in along with an imported project from another application carries media added with timecode but no reel identifier (say, when shots from several unlabeled reels all begin at 0 hour). The catch is that you might not spot such clip conflicts right away, not until you turn Conform Lock Enabled off.

TIP: Overlapping timecode crops up routinely as you work, but it's best handled by editing each clip's embedded reel name, or by sorting media across separate bins.

### Using Clip Conflicts as a Conform Tool

Then again, clip conflicts can frequently be welcome answers in workflows where you have to flip between different versions of one particular clip. Consider an edited timeline made of transcoded QuickTime versions of camera raw original media: if the Media Pool held only the transcoded clips, everything's fine. But say that midway through the work you realize you want the resolution or the extra color latitude of the camera raw version of one specific clip. Import the camera raw version of that single clip and nothing should look any different. Yet should you right-click the clip in the Timeline afterward and pick Conform Lock Enabled to uncheck and switch that setting off, the attention badge ought to show up left of the clip's name in the Timeline. That tells you this clip in the Timeline is rightly registering the link between itself and the two now identically named clips with timecode overlap in the Media Pool. The standing relationship between this timeline clip and the one it's conformed to stays put; the badge merely flags that a second clip now exists in the Media Pool that you could conform this Timeline clip to instead. All that's left is to settle on which one by double-clicking the clip conflict badge and walking through the steps below.

### Resolving Clip Conflicts

Once a clip conflict crops up, deliberate or not, clearing it is genuinely simple. Indeed, this very feature is what the software's name comes from. Relinking a clip's media to resolve a reel conflict goes like this: 1 On any clip in the Timeline, Double-click the “attention” badge shown left of that clip's name. Up comes the Conflict Resolution window, which lists every Media Pool file, of whatever format, carrying matching file names (or reel names) and timecode that overlaps the clip you rightclicked. For each entry the list shows a clip thumbnail, the media's file path on disk, the file name, the starting timecode, the reel name (where present), plus the creation date, helping you judge which of those clips you're really after.

Conflict Resolution window displaying which additional clips share overlapping timecode plus reel information

2 In the list, click whichever entry should be conformed to, then click Apply. That Timeline clip updates to match the media you picked, while the “attention” icon yields to a “resolved” badge marking the conflict as settled. Bear in mind that you can at any point doubleclick the “resolved” badge to swap which Media Pool clip you conform to. The relationship stays dynamic.

### Re-editing Media Directly to the Timeline

If, for any reason, none of the relinking or reconforming approaches above pan out, occasionally the one option left is to swap the troublesome clip in your timeline for a different clip. You might, for instance, need to swap an old version of an effects shot for a newer one, or or swap an offline version of a stock footage shot for a higher quality one, where the snag is a mismatched filename and/or timecode, no reel name, and files that differ entirely in format, frame size, and duration. Here it's fortunate that DaVinci Resolve packs such strong editing tools. Editing gets fuller treatment across Chapter 34 through Chapter 47. While sorting out conform troubles by hand-editing, the replace edit becomes your best ally. You could, say, lean on a replace edit to line a new incoming clip's timing up with the old one's. Or you could reach for a three-point edit, a place on top edit, or even a plain drag and drop edit to drop the new clip into the Timeline so it supplants the old one. The various edit types within DaVinci Resolve are spelled out specifically in Chapter 36, “Editing Basics.”

### How Grades Are Linked to Multiple Timelines

When your project is configured to use Remote versions, any clips pointing at the same file in the Media Pool become linked and share the Remote versions of grades applied to them. As an example, two close-up clips from one take point to the same media file, so they're automatically linked together and share the same remote grades. Clips on Remote versions behave this way across multiple timelines too. Clips on Remote versions that sit in different timelines yet point at the same file in the Media Pool stay linked and share the same remote versions of grades. That's why you can grade one timeline, then bring in a re-edited version via EDL, AAF, or XML, and watch the new timeline pick up every grade from the earlier one automatically. You can, however, override that behavior so one timeline grades independently of the rest. Just pick that timeline, head into the Color page, right-click whatever clip sits in the Thumbnail timeline, and from the contextual menu pick Copy Remote Grades to Local. All grades are duplicated into Local versions, after which any edit you make to that timeline's grades leaves your project's remaining timelines untouched. The topic of Local and Remote versions gets covered more fully in Chapter 142, “Grade Management.”
